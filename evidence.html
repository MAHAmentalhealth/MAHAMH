Evidence & your options.

Understanding a treatment means asking what it may offer, what risks it carries, how strong the evidence is, and what alternatives or additional supports exist.

START HERE

Better information makes better decisions possible.

People can benefit from existing care and still deserve candid conversations about its limits. Before starting, continuing, changing, or stopping a treatment, ask:

  1. What benefits are realistic for someone in my situation, and how were they measured?
  2. What short- and long-term risks or adverse effects are known?
  3. What is uncertain, and whose experiences may be missing from the research?
  4. What other forms of support could be used alongside or instead of this option?
  5. How will we monitor whether this is helping, and what is the plan if it is not?
PSYCHIATRIC DRUGS & INFORMED CONSENT

Read the source. Ask the next question.

FDA-approved labeling describes indications, warnings, adverse reactions, and patient information for specific medicines. Independent resources can help people frame questions about clinical trials, outcomes, and lived experience. Read different kinds of sources together and discuss what applies to you with a qualified professional.

WITHDRAWAL & TAPERING

Careful change takes preparation.

Withdrawal symptoms can be mistaken for a return of an underlying condition, and abrupt discontinuation can cause serious harm. The pace and support needed vary by person and medicine. These links are educational starting points, not personal schedules.

Before making a change

Learn about the medicine and its withdrawal risks, consider timing and support, and discuss a plan with a prescriber who can respond if symptoms emerge. Do not stop abruptly on the basis of a website.

A BROADER RANGE OF SUPPORT

Care extends beyond one modality.

Therapy, peer connection, family and community relationships, sleep, nutrition, physical health, and environmental conditions can all matter. The strength of evidence differs by approach and condition; emerging ideas deserve serious study without being presented as universal answers.

SCIENTIFIC HUMILITY

Uncertainty is useful information.

Studies may measure short-term symptom change while people care about long-term functioning, relationships, and quality of life. Some populations and adverse experiences are underrepresented. We seek sources that name these limits clearly, and we do not treat a promising hypothesis as a settled result.
